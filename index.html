The architecture of sleep

Modern sleep scoring groups sleep into wakefulness, three NREM stages and REM sleep.

Wake

Awake

Transition into sleep or brief awakenings. Brain activity and movement are relatively high.

N1

Light sleep

The transition from wakefulness to sleep. It is relatively easy to awaken from.

N2

Stable sleep

Light-to-moderate sleep with characteristic EEG events such as sleep spindles and K-complexes.

N3

Deep sleep

Slow-wave sleep. It is generally more prominent earlier in the night.

REM

Rapid Eye Movement

REM features active brain patterns, rapid eye movements and normally reduced skeletal-muscle tone. Dreaming can occur in both REM and NREM, although vivid dreams are especially common in REM. REM periods generally become longer later in the night.

Why do we sleep?

There is no single universally accepted explanation. Several complementary theories have been proposed.

01
1982

Two-Process Model — Alexander Borbély

Sleep timing can be understood through two interacting processes: a homeostatic sleep pressure that builds during wakefulness and a circadian process that creates a roughly 24-hour rhythm.

02
1977

Activation-Synthesis Hypothesis — Hobson & McCarley

Proposed that dreams arise when the sleeping brain attempts to make sense of internally generated neural activation, especially during REM sleep.

03
Restoration

Restorative Theory — Ian Oswald

Sleep was proposed to support physical restoration and recovery. Modern research continues to investigate how sleep supports cellular, metabolic, immune and neural maintenance.

04
Evolution

Evolutionary / Adaptive Theory — William C. Dement & others

Adaptive explanations propose that sleep evolved because inactivity at biologically appropriate times can conserve energy and reduce exposure to environmental hazards.

05
1953

Discovery of REM Sleep — Aserinsky & Kleitman

Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman identified periods of rapid eye movements during sleep, helping establish REM as a distinct physiological state.

06
AASM

Modern Staging System

Modern clinical scoring divides sleep into Wake, N1, N2, N3 and REM using physiological signals such as EEG, EOG and EMG. The AASM publishes standardized scoring rules.

07
Memory

Memory Consolidation

Sleep supports learning and memory by helping process, stabilize and integrate information acquired while awake. Different sleep states may contribute in different ways.

08
Synapses

Synaptic Homeostasis Hypothesis

One influential hypothesis suggests sleep helps restore synaptic balance after the accumulation of neural connections and activity during wakefulness.

How scientists measure sleep today

Clinical sleep studies combine multiple physiological signals. Consumer devices usually estimate sleep indirectly.

EEG — ElectroencephalographyMeasures electrical brain activity and is central to distinguishing sleep stages.
EOG — ElectrooculographyRecords eye movements, helping identify REM and other sleep characteristics.
EMG — ElectromyographyMeasures muscle activity; reduced muscle tone is a defining feature of REM sleep.
ECG / heart-rate signalsProvides cardiovascular information that can complement sleep staging and clinical assessment.
Respiratory sensorsAirflow, breathing effort and oxygen saturation can be monitored, especially when sleep-disordered breathing is suspected.
ActigraphyMovement-based monitoring can estimate sleep/wake patterns over longer periods.
PPG & wearable sensorsWatches and rings may use optical pulse signals, movement and other sensors to estimate sleep metrics.
Polysomnography (PSG)A comprehensive clinical sleep study combining signals such as EEG, EOG, EMG and breathing measurements.

Sleep-health instructions

Use these as general sleep-hygiene guidance, not as a substitute for medical care.

Keep a schedule

Try to keep consistent sleep and wake times, including on days off.

Protect your sleep environment

Keep the bedroom comfortable, dark and quiet, and reduce disruptive light and noise.

Watch stimulants

Caffeine and bright light late in the day can interfere with sleep timing for some people.

Move during the day

Regular physical activity can support healthy sleep, while very intense exercise immediately before bed may not suit everyone.

Don't obsess over scores

Wearable sleep-stage estimates have limitations. Look at trends rather than treating a nightly score as a diagnosis.

Know when to seek help

Persistent insomnia, loud snoring with breathing pauses, severe daytime sleepiness or other concerning symptoms deserve professional evaluation.
